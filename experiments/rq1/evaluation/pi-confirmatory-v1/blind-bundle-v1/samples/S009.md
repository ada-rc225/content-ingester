# Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and the Power Iteration

## 1. Why eigenpairs matter

A matrix describes how components of a vector are combined. Usually, multiplying a vector by a matrix changes both its length and its direction. An eigenvector is a special exception: multiplication changes its length, but not its direction. For a square matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $v$ is an eigenvector if

$$Av = \lambda v,$$

where the scalar $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue. The vector and scalar together form an eigenpair. The equation says that the action of $A$ on $v$ is equivalent to scaling $v$ by $\lambda$.

This idea has a useful mechanical-engineering interpretation. If a simplified model maps a displacement or state vector to a response through a matrix, an eigenvector can represent a pattern that the matrix preserves, while the eigenvalue measures the associated amplification or reversal. This is only a bridge to intuition: the meaning of a matrix depends on the model, and not every matrix is a stiffness, mass, or dynamical matrix.

For a nonzero eigenvector, multiplying it by any nonzero scalar gives another eigenvector for the same eigenvalue. In particular, if $v$ is an eigenvector, then $-v$ is also one. Therefore, an eigenvector has a direction, but its sign is not uniquely determined. Numerical algorithms may return either sign on different runs or for slightly different starting vectors without making an error.

## 2. Finding and interpreting eigenvalues

Rearranging the eigenvalue equation gives

$$ (A-\lambda I)v=0. $$

A nonzero solution exists only when $A-\lambda I$ is singular, so eigenvalues satisfy the characteristic equation

$$\det(A-\lambda I)=0.$$

For small matrices, this equation can sometimes be solved directly. For larger matrices, forming and solving the characteristic polynomial is generally not the preferred computational approach. Iterative methods instead use matrix-vector products to approximate selected eigenpairs.

A real symmetric matrix has especially useful properties. All its eigenvalues are real, and it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Thus, for an $n$ by $n$ symmetric matrix, we can write

$$A=Q\Lambda Q^T,$$

where the columns of $Q$ are orthonormal eigenvectors and $\Lambda$ is diagonal with the corresponding eigenvalues. Any vector $x$ can then be expressed as

$$x=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i v_i.$$

The coefficient $c_i=v_i^Tx$ is the projection of $x$ onto the eigenvector $v_i$.

The dominant eigenvalue for power iteration means the eigenvalue with largest magnitude:

$$|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\geq\cdots\geq|\lambda_n|.$$

This is not necessarily the largest algebraic eigenvalue. For example, the eigenvalues $-8$, $3$, and $1$ have largest algebraic value $3$, but the dominant-by-magnitude eigenvalue is $-8$. Power iteration targets magnitude, so it approaches the eigenvector associated with $-8$ in this example, while successive vector signs alternate.

## 3. Deriving power iteration

Start with a nonzero vector $x_0$ and repeatedly multiply by $A$:

$$y_k=Ax_k, \qquad x_{k+1}=\frac{y_k}{\|y_k\|}.$$

Normalization prevents the vector from becoming extremely large or small. To see why the direction tends to a particular eigenvector, express the initial vector in the orthonormal eigenbasis:

$$x_0=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i v_i.$$

After $k$ unnormalized multiplications,

$$A^kx_0=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\lambda_i^k v_i.$$

Factor out $\lambda_1^k$:

$$A^kx_0=\lambda_1^k\left(c_1v_1+\sum_{i=2}^n c_i\left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^k v_i\right).$$

If $c_1\neq 0$ and $|\lambda_i/\lambda_1|<1$ for every $i>1$, the unwanted terms shrink as $k$ increases. The normalized vector therefore approaches $v_1$ or $-v_1$. If $\lambda_1<0$, the unnormalized direction changes sign at each multiplication; normalization does not remove that sign alternation.

The usual convergence assumptions are therefore: the matrix has a unique eigenvalue of largest magnitude, the starting vector has a nonzero projection onto its eigenvector, and the iteration does not encounter a zero product before convergence. For real symmetric matrices, these assumptions give a particularly clear explanation because the eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis.

The asymptotic error factor is approximately

$$\left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|.$$

Equivalently, the error decreases like this ratio raised to the iteration count. A small spectral gap in magnitude means that $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ is close to one, so convergence is slow. A large separation produces faster convergence. “Spectral gap” can be discussed as a difference or a ratio; for power iteration, the ratio is the direct asymptotic factor.

## 4. Eigenvalue estimates: Rayleigh quotient and residual

Once $x_k$ approximates an eigenvector, the Rayleigh quotient estimates its eigenvalue:

$$\rho(x_k)=\frac{x_k^TAx_k}{x_k^Tx_k}.$$

For a unit vector, this simplifies to $x_k^TAx_k$. In a symmetric problem, the Rayleigh quotient is real and lies between the smallest and largest algebraic eigenvalues. It should not be confused with the magnitude of the eigenvalue. If the target eigenvalue is negative, the quotient should approach a negative number.

A stronger diagnostic is the residual

$$r_k=Ax_k-\rho(x_k)x_k,$$

usually monitored using $\|r_k\|$. A small residual means that the computed pair $(\rho(x_k),x_k)$ nearly satisfies the eigenvalue equation. A vector can appear visually stable while still having a poor eigenvalue estimate, so the residual is useful for deciding when to stop.

For a symmetric matrix, once $x$ is normalized, compute the product once and use it for both estimates:

```python
import numpy as np

def power_iteration(A, x0, tol=1e-10, max_iter=1000):
    A = np.asarray(A, dtype=float)
    x = np.asarray(x0, dtype=float).copy()
    if A.ndim != 2 or A.shape[0] != A.shape[1]:
        raise ValueError("A must be square")
    if x.shape != (A.shape[0],):
        raise ValueError("x0 has the wrong shape")
    norm = np.linalg.norm(x)
    if norm == 0:
        raise ValueError("x0 must be nonzero")
    x /= norm

    for iteration in range(1, max_iter + 1):
        y = A @ x
        y_norm = np.linalg.norm(y)
        if y_norm == 0:
            raise ValueError("iteration broke down: A @ x is zero")
        x = y / y_norm
        Ax = A @ x
        eigenvalue = x @ Ax
        residual = np.linalg.norm(Ax - eigenvalue * x)
        if residual < tol:
            return eigenvalue, x, iteration, residual

    return eigenvalue, x, max_iter, residual

A = np.array([[4.0, 1.0], [1.0, 3.0]])
value, vector, iterations, residual = power_iteration(A, [1.0, 0.2])
print(value, vector, iterations, residual)
```

The returned eigenvector is normalized to unit Euclidean length. Its sign is arbitrary, so compare vectors using either $\|x-v\|$ or $\|x+v\|$, whichever is smaller. In production code, it is also sensible to check that the input intended to be symmetric satisfies $A\approx A^T$ when symmetry is required by the analysis.

## 5. Failure modes and limitations

A zero starting vector cannot be normalized and contains no directional information. More subtly, a nonzero starting vector can have zero projection onto the dominant eigenvector. In exact arithmetic, if $c_1=0$, the dominant component is never created by multiplication, so the iteration may converge to the largest-magnitude eigenvector present in the initial vector instead. With floating-point roundoff, an exactly missing projection may be perturbed, but relying on that is not good practice.

The iteration can also break down when $Ax_k=0$. This occurs if the current vector is in the nullspace. It is not merely a normalization inconvenience: the algorithm has no next direction. A zero or singular matrix deserves special handling.

If two eigenvalues tie for largest magnitude, such as $\lambda_1=2$ and $\lambda_2=-2$, the strict dominance assumption fails. The relative contributions do not decay, and the vectors may oscillate or fail to settle on one eigenvector. Even without an exact tie, a small gap causes many iterations. A negative dominant eigenvalue commonly causes alternating signs, whereas a pair with nearly equal opposite magnitudes can cause especially persistent oscillation.

The clean derivation above uses a real symmetric matrix. For a non-symmetric matrix, eigenvalues may be complex, eigenvectors may not be orthogonal, and the matrix may not even have a complete eigenvector basis. Power iteration can still work when there is a unique dominant eigenvalue with suitable eigenvectors, but the symmetric convergence explanation no longer applies directly. Defective matrices and strong non-normality can produce transient behaviour that is not predicted well by a simple orthogonal expansion. For complex problems, normalization and the Rayleigh quotient must also use conjugate transposes.

Finally, power iteration finds one extreme eigenpair, normally the largest magnitude one. It does not directly find the largest algebraic eigenvalue when a more negative eigenvalue has larger magnitude. Shifts, deflation, inverse iteration, or a library eigensolver may be more appropriate when a different part of the spectrum is needed.

## 6. A practical workflow

For a new symmetric matrix, first confirm its dimensions and approximate symmetry. Choose a nonzero starting vector, often with random entries, and normalize it. Repeatedly perform a matrix-vector product and normalization. At each step calculate the Rayleigh quotient and residual. Stop based on the residual rather than on a fixed number of iterations alone. Record the iteration count and inspect whether the residual decreases; a plateau often signals a small spectral gap, a failed assumption, or an implementation mistake.

In a mechanical model, interpret the result only after checking what the matrix represents. An eigenvector may describe a mode shape or a preserved computational direction, while the eigenvalue may have units or physical meaning determined by the governing equations. The algorithm supplies a numerical eigenpair; physical interpretation requires the model and its scaling.

## 7. Exercises and worked solutions

### Exercise 1 — concept_check

A real symmetric matrix has eigenvalues $-10$, $4$, and $2$. Which eigenvalue does ordinary power iteration target, and what qualitative sign behaviour should be expected?

**Worked solution.** It targets $-10$, because $|-10|=10$ is greater than $|4|=4$ and $|2|=2$. The dominant eigenvalue is negative, so the unnormalized iterates reverse direction on each multiplication. After normalization, the vector typically alternates between approximations to $v$ and $-v$. The Rayleigh quotient approaches $-10$, not $10$ and not the largest algebraic eigenvalue, which is $4$.

### Exercise 2 — hand_calculation

Let

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_0=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}.$$

Perform two power-iteration multiplications without normalization, then identify the limiting eigenvector direction. Include a consistency check using the eigenvalue equation.

**Worked solution.** First,

$$Ax_0=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}.$$

Second,

$$A^2x_0=A\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}10\\6\end{bmatrix}.$$

The eigenvalues are $4$ and $2$, with eigenvectors proportional to $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,-1)^T$, respectively. Since the ratio $4/2$ exceeds one, repeated multiplication approaches the direction $(1,1)^T$. The computed vector $(10,6)^T$ already points closer to that direction than $(3,1)^T$.

For a consistency check, apply $A$ to the proposed limiting direction:

$$A\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\4\end{bmatrix}=4\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}.$$

Thus the direction is an eigenvector and its eigenvalue is $4$, confirming the iteration result.

### Exercise 3 — code_diagnostic

A student writes `x = A @ x` repeatedly but never normalizes `x`, and then reports the final vector as the eigenvector. What is wrong, and what two changes should be made?

**Worked solution.** Without normalization, the vector magnitude generally grows approximately like $|\lambda_1|^k$, which can overflow or become numerically inconvenient. The direction may still contain useful information, but the code should normalize after every multiplication. The student should also estimate the eigenvalue with the Rayleigh quotient and calculate the residual $\|Ax-\rho(x)x\|$ before declaring convergence. A small residual, rather than the size of the vector or a fixed iteration count, is the relevant accuracy check.
