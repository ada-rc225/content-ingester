# Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors, and the Power Iteration


## Eigenpairs and the characteristic equation

An eigenvector is a non-zero direction that a matrix sends back onto itself. For a real square matrix $A$, a non-zero vector $v$ is an eigenvector when

$$Av=\lambda v.$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is its associated eigenvalue. The non-zero requirement matters: the zero vector satisfies the equation for every scalar and therefore carries no information about a special direction. In a mechanical computation, it is useful to think of the vector as a pattern of coordinates and the matrix as a transformation of that pattern. This is a bounded analogy for visualising direction; it does not by itself assert a physical mode or a measured property.

Rearranging the eigenpair equation gives $(A-\lambda I)v=0$. A non-zero solution exists only when the matrix $A-\lambda I$ is singular, so eigenvalues satisfy the characteristic equation

$$\det(A-\lambda I)=0.$$

Here $I$ is the identity matrix of the same dimension as $A$. For a small matrix, this equation can be useful for an exact calculation. For a large matrix, repeatedly applying matrix-vector products can be a preferable numerical strategy to explicitly forming a characteristic polynomial. These are different computational approaches, not an assertion that iteration is an algebraic replacement for the determinant equation.

For example, the matrix

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$$

has characteristic equation $(4-\lambda)(2-\lambda)-1=0$, which simplifies to $\lambda^2-6\lambda+7=0$. Its eigenvalues are $3+\sqrt{2}$ and $3-\sqrt{2}$. The larger magnitude is $3+\sqrt{2}$, so this matrix provides a useful test of an iterative method.


## Dominant modes in symmetric matrices

A real symmetric matrix satisfies $A=A^T$. In this setting, the eigenvectors can be chosen as an orthonormal basis. Put these vectors into the columns of $Q$ and put the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$. Then

$$A=Q\Lambda Q^T,\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).$$

Every vector can be resolved into these directions:

$$x=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i v_i,\qquad c_i=v_i^Tx.$$

This decomposition is the key to understanding power iteration. It separates a general starting vector into modal components without requiring us to find those components explicitly in the algorithm.

### What dominant means

Order the eigenvalues by magnitude:

$$|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\ge\cdots\ge|\lambda_n|.$$

The strict first inequality says that one eigenvalue is uniquely dominant in magnitude. It does not say that $\lambda_1$ is the most positive eigenvalue. If two eigenvalues are $5$ and $-8$, the power method targets $-8$, because $|-8|>|5|$. The direction associated with a negative dominant eigenvalue may reverse sign from one step to the next, but a vector and its negative represent the same eigendirection.

This distinction is important when interpreting a computed result. A large positive number is not automatically the target, and a changing sign is not automatically a failed computation. The magnitude controls which component grows fastest; the sign controls possible alternation.


## Deriving power iteration and its convergence

Start with a non-zero vector $x_0$ and apply the matrix repeatedly. Using the eigenbasis expansion gives

$$A^kx_0=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\lambda_i^k v_i
=\lambda_1^k\left(c_1v_1+\sum_{i=2}^n c_i\left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^k v_i\right).$$

The dominant component is multiplied by $\lambda_1^k$. Each subordinate component is scaled relative to it by $(\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k$. If $c_1=v_1^Tx_0\ne0$ and the strict magnitude gap holds, these ratios become small as $k$ increases. The vector therefore approaches the dominant eigendirection, up to scale and possibly alternating sign.

Raw multiplication is numerically inconvenient because the size of a vector can grow or shrink rapidly. Power iteration controls that scale at every step:

$$y_{k+1}=Ax_k,\qquad x_{k+1}=\frac{y_{k+1}}{\|y_{k+1}\|_2}.$$

Normalisation changes the length but not the direction of a non-zero $y_{k+1}$. The denominator must be non-zero, so a zero product is a breakdown rather than a value to divide by.

The usual convergence statement is conditional. The matrix must be real and symmetric, the dominant magnitude must be unique, the starting vector must have a non-zero projection on the dominant eigenvector, and no iterate may produce $Ax_k=0$. Under those assumptions, direction error generally decreases asymptotically at a rate governed by

$$\left|\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\right|^k.$$

This is an asymptotic rate, not an unconditional finite-iteration error bound. A ratio close to one means that many iterations may be needed even though convergence eventually occurs.


## Estimates, residuals, and algorithm behaviour

Once an iterate has been normalised, estimate its eigenvalue with the Rayleigh quotient

$$\rho(x_k)=\frac{x_k^TAx_k}{x_k^Tx_k}.$$

For a unit vector, this reduces to $\rho(x_k)=x_k^TAx_k$. In the symmetric setting, as $x_k$ approaches $v_1$, the quotient estimates $\lambda_1$. The denominator should still be retained when the vector is not known to be normalised.

A stronger diagnostic is the eigenpair residual

$$r_k=Ax_k-\rho(x_k)x_k.$$

An exact eigenpair has zero residual. The norm $\|r_k\|_2$ measures how closely the computed vector and scalar satisfy the eigenpair equation. Comparing successive vectors alone can be misleading: a sign flip can make two equally good representations look far apart. The residual avoids treating that sign ambiguity as a separate physical or mathematical direction.

### An implementation sequence

A reliable implementation first converts the inputs to floating NumPy arrays, checks that $A$ is square, checks that $x_0$ is one-dimensional with a compatible length, rejects a zero initial vector, and normalises $x$. It also requires a positive tolerance and validates that `max_iterations` is a positive integer before entering the loop. Each loop applies the matrix-vector product, checks for a zero product before division, normalises, computes the Rayleigh estimate and residual, and returns early when the residual norm is at most the tolerance. Otherwise it returns after the maximum number of iterations.

```python
import numpy as np

def power_iteration(A, x0, epsilon=1e-8, max_iterations=1000):
    A = np.asarray(A, dtype=float)
    x = np.asarray(x0, dtype=float)
    if A.ndim != 2 or A.shape[0] != A.shape[1]:
        raise ValueError("A must be square")
    if x.ndim != 1 or x.shape[0] != A.shape[0]:
        raise ValueError("x0 must be one-dimensional with compatible length")
    if np.linalg.norm(x) == 0:
        raise ValueError("x0 must be non-zero")
    if epsilon <= 0:
        raise ValueError("epsilon must be positive")
    if not isinstance(max_iterations, (int, np.integer)) or isinstance(max_iterations, (bool, np.bool_)) or max_iterations <= 0:
        raise ValueError("max_iterations must be a positive integer")
    x = x / np.linalg.norm(x)
    for iteration in range(1, max_iterations + 1):
        y = A @ x
        y_norm = np.linalg.norm(y)
        if y_norm == 0:
            raise ValueError("power iteration broke down: zero product")
        x = y / y_norm
        eigenvalue = float(x @ A @ x)
        residual = A @ x - eigenvalue * x
        residual_norm = float(np.linalg.norm(residual))
        if residual_norm <= epsilon:
            return eigenvalue, x, residual_norm, iteration
    return eigenvalue, x, residual_norm, max_iterations
```


## Failure cases and a NumPy implementation

Several cases explain why the assumptions belong next to the convergence claim. If $v_1^Tx_0=0$, the initial vector contains no dominant eigendirection component. In exact arithmetic, multiplying by the matrix cannot create that missing eigencomponent, so the method does not converge to $v_1$ as an ordinary slow run. A different starting vector is needed.

If $|\lambda_1|=|\lambda_2|$, the dominant magnitude is repeated and a unique eigenvector need not be selected. This is different from the case where $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ is merely close to one: then the dominant direction is unique, but convergence can be very slow. Keeping these cases separate helps diagnose a run rather than blaming the stopping tolerance for every difficulty.

The clean derivation above relies on a real symmetric matrix and its orthonormal eigenbasis. Power iteration can apply to some non-symmetric matrices, but the orthogonal decomposition is not generally available. Defective matrices, complex dominant eigenvalues, and non-normal behaviour require additional care and are outside this lesson's scope. Thus the implementation can be demonstrated more broadly than the convergence explanation, but the symmetric assumptions should not be silently dropped.

For the worked matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}4&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ with $x_0=(1,1)$, the exact values are $3+\sqrt{2}$ and $3-\sqrt{2}$. The first is dominant by magnitude. Running the implementation estimates $3+\sqrt{2}$ and produces a small residual; the sign of the returned eigenvector is not required to match a particular orientation. In engineering computation, this is the useful interpretation: the estimated scalar and direction should be assessed together through the residual.

The method is therefore a compact chain: validate inputs, normalise the initial direction, multiply, check for breakdown, normalise again, estimate the scalar, evaluate the residual, and stop either on tolerance or on the positive iteration limit. Each step has a numerical purpose and corresponds directly to the mathematical reasoning.

### Reading a numerical run

When inspecting output, record the iteration count as well as the scalar estimate and residual norm. An early return means the residual criterion was reached; a return at the configured limit means the fallback was used. Those outcomes answer different questions, even when the final vectors look similar. A residual that is small relative to the scale of the matrix supports the eigenpair equation, while a large residual signals that more iterations, a different start, or a more suitable method may be needed.

The starting vector is therefore part of the experiment, not merely a coding detail. Two non-zero starts can produce different transient paths, and an unfortunate start can hide the dominant direction entirely. In a symmetric problem, the eigenbasis explanation makes this transparent: the coefficients determine which components are available to be amplified. Normalising the start removes arbitrary length while retaining those directional coefficients up to a common scale.

For a mechanical engineer, this viewpoint separates three layers of interpretation. The matrix calculation supplies the numerical transformation, the eigenpair supplies a special direction and scale factor, and any modal language is a chosen context for reasoning about those objects. Keeping the layers separate makes it easier to transfer the method to another matrix problem without importing claims that the calculation itself has not established.

### Exercise 1 — Concept check

A matrix has eigenvalues $5$ and $-8$. Which eigenvalue does power iteration target under the magnitude-gap assumption? If successive normalised vectors alternate between $v$ and $-v$, is that alone evidence of failure? Explain briefly.


### Worked solution

Power iteration targets $-8$ because its magnitude is $8$, larger than $5$. Alternation between $v$ and $-v$ is compatible with a negative dominant eigenvalue. The two vectors describe the same eigendirection, so sign alternation alone is not failure; a residual should be used to assess the eigenpair.
